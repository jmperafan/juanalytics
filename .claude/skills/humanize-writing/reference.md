# Voice reference — the gold standard

The anchor for this skill is **Chapter 13, "Data Governance," of *Fundamentals
of Analytics Engineering*** (Juan is a co-author). The full chapter PDF sits in
this folder for local reading — `fundamentalsofanalyticsengineering-271-282.pdf`
— and is **gitignored on purpose**: the publisher holds copyright, this repo is
public, don't commit it. The short excerpts below are here for calibration.

When a draft is in doubt, read a strong passage from this chapter and ask
whether the draft sounds like the same person wrote it.

## What the target voice does

**1. Concede the unglamorous or hard truth, then make the case anyway.**
> "Data governance is not the sexiest topic, but it is what separates mature data teams from the rest."

> "In fact, we secretly believe most people reading this book will skip this chapter after reading the title. However, we urge you to stay a bit longer."

It names the reader's actual objection instead of pretending the topic is
thrilling. That candor is what buys the next paragraph's attention.

**2. Define a thing by its consequence, not by a dictionary.**
> "it is not about creating new dashboards or pipelines but ensuring that the existing ones do not break."

Contrast with the chapter's own weaker formal definition ("a comprehensive and
disciplined approach to managing and protecting an organization's data
assets") — the consequence version is the one a reader remembers.

**3. Anchor every abstraction to a mundane, specific example.**
> "…paying your employees every month or ensuring that there is fresh coffee every morning."

> allergens "(e.g., nuts or shrimp)"

> "Imagine parents who buy a toy for their child. Who is the customer? The parents, the child, or both?"

Not "a relatable example" in the abstract — coffee, shrimp, a kid's toy.
Concrete nouns do the persuading.

**4. Talk to the reader and say what they're probably thinking.**
> "So far, the concept of data governance might feel overwhelming or a bit fuzzy. If so, there is no need to worry. Nobody is going to ask you for a textbook definition."

Second person, present tense, addresses the doubt directly.

**5. Let the asides be wry and self-aware.**
> "Ironically, it takes a lot of hard work and experience to be fully replaceable."

> "Honestly, we might retire before the huge market gap for experienced analytics experts closes."

**6. State stakes bluntly and briefly.**
> "bad data can lead to erroneous decisions, uncountable losses, or even death."

Short sentence, hard last word, no cushioning.

**7. Pose the hard question instead of asserting that something is complex.**
> "When exactly do you become a customer? Is it when you enter a shop, when you show any intent of purchasing, when you pay, when you receive the goods…?"

The draft-writer's reflex is "defining a customer is surprisingly complex." The
chapter instead *asks the questions* and lets the reader feel the complexity.

**8. End without a victory lap.**
> "Data governance refers to any task you must do to make your data compliant, secure, accurate, available, and useful. Even though organizations often ignore it, it sets mature data teams apart."

The summary restates the thesis in one breath and stops.

## Where even this chapter slips — don't imitate these

The same chapter has sections that drift into the generic register this skill
exists to remove, most visibly "Engaging stakeholders and fostering
collaboration" and "Resistance to change and adoption":

> "Engaging stakeholders and fostering collaboration is crucial to ensure the successful implementation of IT projects."

Three tells in one sentence — *fostering*, *crucial*, *ensure* — and not one
concrete noun.

> "Effective communication plays a vital role in engaging stakeholders and fostering collaboration:" — used as a **bold list header**, with body text that only restates it.

> "…we will delve into these challenges and provide insights on overcoming them."

The lesson: competent, published, edited prose still slides into this register
whenever the writer reaches a subheading they don't have a specific point
about. The fix is never wordsmithing — it's finding the concrete claim, or
cutting the section. If a passage in a draft reads like these, treat "which
gold-standard trait is missing" as the diagnostic: usually 2 (consequence) and
3 (concrete anchor).
